Introduction to Macroeconomics

When news anchors report that "the economy grew by 2.4% last quarter" or that "the Fed raised interest rates," they're speaking the language of macroeconomics. While microeconomics focuses on the decisions of individual consumers and firms, macroeconomics studies the economy as a whole — the forces that determine total output, employment, and the price level across an entire nation.

What Is Macroeconomics?

Macroeconomics is concerned with aggregate outcomes: the total production of goods and services in the economy, the fraction of the labor force that is unemployed, the rate at which prices are rising, and the pace of long-run economic growth.

These aggregates matter enormously for people's lives. When the economy contracts sharply — as it did during the 2008 financial crisis or the 2020 COVID recession — millions of workers lose jobs, businesses fail, and household wealth evaporates. When the economy grows steadily, living standards rise over time, opportunities expand, and governments have the resources to fund public services.

Understanding why these fluctuations occur, and what policymakers can (and can't) do about them, is the central project of macroeconomics.

The Three Key Macroeconomic Variables

Three variables receive most of the attention in macroeconomics:

1. Gross Domestic Product (GDP)

GDP is the total market value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders in a given period. It's the most widely used measure of an economy's size and health.

When GDP rises, the economy is expanding — firms are producing more, workers are employed, incomes are growing. When GDP falls for two consecutive quarters, economists say the economy is in a recession.

2. Unemployment

The unemployment rate measures the fraction of the labor force that is actively looking for work but cannot find a job. A high unemployment rate signals that the economy is not fully utilizing its human resources — a costly waste with real consequences for workers and their families.

Not all unemployment is the same, however:

  • Frictional unemployment arises from the normal time it takes workers to move between jobs.
  • Structural unemployment occurs when workers' skills no longer match the jobs available — a longer-term mismatch caused by technological change or industry shifts.
  • Cyclical unemployment rises and falls with the business cycle, spiking during recessions.

Economists consider an economy at "full employment" when only frictional and structural unemployment remain — there is no cyclical unemployment. This level is sometimes called the natural rate of unemployment.

3. The Price Level and Inflation

Inflation is a sustained rise in the overall price level. When prices rise broadly, each dollar buys less than it used to — purchasing power erodes.

The Consumer Price Index (CPI) is the most common measure of inflation. It tracks the cost of a fixed "basket" of goods and services that a typical household purchases, and measures how that cost changes over time.

Moderate, stable inflation (around 2% per year) is generally considered healthy. High or unpredictable inflation creates problems: it redistributes income in arbitrary ways, discourages saving and long-term investment, and makes economic planning difficult.

Deflation — falling prices — sounds appealing but can be dangerous. When people expect prices to fall further, they delay purchases, which reduces spending and can push the economy into a deflationary spiral.

The Business Cycle

Real-world economies don't grow at a steady pace. They fluctuate — periods of expansion are followed by downturns. This rhythmic pattern is the business cycle.

A typical business cycle has four phases:

  1. Expansion — Output rises, unemployment falls, business and consumer confidence grows.
  2. Peak — The economy reaches maximum output before beginning to contract.
  3. Contraction (Recession) — Output falls, unemployment rises, investment slows.
  4. Trough — The lowest point of the cycle, after which recovery begins.

Business cycles are irregular — no two are alike in length or severity. The Great Depression of the 1930s lasted over a decade. The 2001 recession lasted just eight months. Macroeconomists study what causes these fluctuations and how policy can moderate them.

Long-Run Growth vs. Short-Run Fluctuations

One of the most useful distinctions in macroeconomics is between the long run and the short run.

In the long run, what matters is an economy's productive capacity — the quantity and quality of labor, physical capital, and the level of technology. Long-run growth is driven by investment, education, innovation, and institutions that support market exchange.

In the short run, actual output can deviate from this potential. Aggregate demand — the total spending in the economy — can fall short, leaving workers unemployed and factories idle. Or it can temporarily exceed potential, pushing up inflation.

The tools of macroeconomic policy — fiscal policy (government spending and taxes) and monetary policy (control of the money supply and interest rates) — are primarily aimed at smoothing short-run fluctuations without sacrificing long-run growth.

Macroeconomic Models

Like all sciences, economics relies on models — simplified representations of reality designed to illuminate key relationships. A model is never a perfect description of the world; it's a tool for thinking clearly.

The main models we'll use in this course include:

  • The Aggregate Demand–Aggregate Supply (AD-AS) model — the workhorse framework for analyzing short-run fluctuations and the effects of policy.
  • The Solow Growth Model — a framework for understanding the sources of long-run economic growth.
  • The IS-LM model — used to analyze how fiscal and monetary policy interact.
  • The Phillips Curve — describes the short-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment.

Each model abstracts from details to focus on the mechanisms that matter most for the question at hand.

Why Macroeconomics Is Hard

Macroeconomics is intellectually challenging for several reasons:

Economies are complex. Millions of households, firms, banks, and governments make decisions simultaneously, and these decisions interact in ways that are hard to predict.

We can't run controlled experiments. Physicists can isolate a system and test how it behaves. Macroeconomists must analyze an economy as it runs — often with incomplete data, only one version of history, and policy interventions that change the thing being studied.

Cause and effect are often unclear. Did the stimulus package end the recession, or would the recovery have happened anyway? These counterfactual questions are difficult to answer with certainty.

Despite these challenges, macroeconomics has generated durable insights about how economies work — and what policies help or harm. By the end of this course, you'll have a framework for reading economic news critically, evaluating policy proposals, and understanding the forces that shape your own economic circumstances.
