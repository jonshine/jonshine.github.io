The Inflation Basket

Think about the last time you went grocery shopping. The fruits and vegetables piled high, shelves stuffed with boxes of cereal, drinks neatly stacked up all down the row. Passing through each aisle, weighing all your options.

When you made your selections, what exactly did you consider? Did you come with a list so you could make a favorite recipe? Were you out of paper towels and dish soap? Were you planning for a party and needed food and drinks to entertain? Did you come in with a budget that you need to stretch into meals for the week?

As you well know, each item that made it into your cart has a price. When you check out and look at your receipt, you see what your basket of goods costs. But be honest, how much did you pay attention to the cost of everything you picked out? Do you check the price of your favorite snack every time or do you just toss it in your cart?

As economists, one of the most direct measures we can get from the economy is paying attention to the price of goods and services. Using your grocery cart as an example, your cart may total out to be $100 this week but come back in a few months with the exact same items in the basket, and likely you'll pay a different price. If the total cost of the same goods goes up, that's what we refer to as inflation. If total cost goes down, that's deflation.

If I'm still earning the same amount of money at my job, when I face higher prices all of a sudden, my paycheck doesn't go as far, and I feel poorer by comparison. On the flipside, if the total price goes down, I am now richer by comparison because I have a little extra money leftover that I can use to spend on something else.

Part 1: Fill Your Basket

For this case study, we are going to measure our own personal inflation rate by filling up a virtual shopping cart at HEB.com and comparing the total price of all the items from beginning of the semester to the end.

To get a good measure, here are some requirements you need to meet to fill up your basket.

  • Add items into your cart to be able to make 5 different meals.
  • Pretend you will host a group of friends over on the weekend to watch a game/movie. Buy snacks, drinks, and party supplies.
  • Pick at least 10 items from the "Everyday Essentials," "Health & Beauty," "Home & Outdoor," "Baby & Kids," and "Pets" categories. (A total of 10 items, not 10 from each category.)
  • Once you've added all your items, navigate to your shopping cart and save a copy of the page by printing to PDF.
  • Upload your PDF to the Inflation Assignment Folder on D2L.

We will revisit this case study later in the semester when we learn about inflation in more detail.

Note: While you don't have to log in or register with HEB to complete this assignment, if you choose to you can save your items to a list — that way when it's time to fill your basket up again to compare prices, you don't have to add in the items one by one all over again.

Part 2: Revisit and Reflect

As our class wraps up, it's time for us to revisit the Inflation Case Study we started back at the beginning of the semester. This week I want you to go back to HEB.com and fill up your virtual shopping cart with the exact same items that you did before. (If for some reason an item is no longer in stock, do your best to find a similar item that can serve as a close substitute.) Like before, print to PDF and upload a copy to the Inflation Case Study assignment folder.

Once you've done that, come back to this discussion topic and share with the class your response to the following prompts.

  1. Calculate the inflation rate for your own personal basket of goods and share your findings.
  2. Did you have any items that increased in price? If so, which three items increased by the most?
  3. Did you have any items that decreased in price? If so, which three items decreased by the most?
  4. Did any items keep the same price? If so, give an example.
  5. Share with the class your thoughts about your findings. Were there any changes that were larger than you expected? Smaller than expected?
